# A Working Grammar of Nandi

**Preliminary Edition — Community Draft**

---

## Preface

This grammar documents the Nandi language as currently verified in the Kutiit dictionary and grammar corpus. It is a **working grammar** — it covers what has been documented and verified by native speakers, and honestly notes where documentation is incomplete.

It is not a final grammar. It is a foundation that will grow as more is documented.

This document has two parts:

1. **Grammar** — the rules of the language
2. **Workbook** — exercises for learners

Corrections and additions are welcome.

# PART ONE: GRAMMAR

## Chapter 1: Introduction

### 1.1 The Nandi Language

Nandi is a Nilotic language of the Kalenjin group, spoken in Kenya. It is closely related to Kipsigis, Keiyo, Tugen, Marakwet, Pokot, Sabaot, and Terik.

### 1.2 About This Grammar

This grammar is based on:

- Community verification by native speakers
- The Kutiit dictionary corpus (900+ entries)
- Structural patterns observed across documented verbs and nouns

Where documentation is incomplete, this is noted explicitly.

## Chapter 2: Sound System

### 2.1 Consonants

Nandi has consonants including:

- Voiceless explosives: /p, t, c, k/
- Fricative: /s/
- Nasals: /m, n, ny, ŋ/
- Liquids: /l, r/
- Semi-vowels: /w, y/

### 2.2 Vowels

Nandi has a vowel system with length distinctions:

| Short | Long |
|-------|------|
| a | aa |
| e | ee |
| i | ii |
| o | oo |
| u | uu |

Vowel length is meaningful and must be learned as part of each word.

### 2.3 Tone

Nandi has lexical tone — tone patterns are part of each word's identity and must be learned individually.

**Note:** The tone system is not yet fully documented in this grammar. Future editions will include lexical tone patterns and grammatical tone marking (case distinction via tone).

## Chapter 3: Nouns

### 3.1 Primary and Secondary Forms

Every Nandi noun has two forms:

| Form | Function | Example |
|------|----------|---------|
| **Primary (indefinite)** | General reference, "a X" | `chii` — a person |
| **Secondary (definite)** | Specific reference, "the X" | `chito` — the person |

The definite form is the more commonly used form in speech.

### 3.2 Number

Nouns distinguish singular and plural. Plural forms are often suppletive — that is, the plural stem differs from the singular stem.

**Example: `chii` (person)**

| | Indefinite | Definite |
|---|-----------|----------|
| **Singular** | chii | chito |
| **Plural** | piich | piik |

The plural stem `pii-` is completely different from the singular stem `chi-`. This is common in Nandi.

### 3.3 Noun Classes (Plural Formation)

Nouns fall into classes based on their plural suffixes. Six classes have been documented:

| Class | Plural Pattern | Example |
|-------|----------------|---------|
| 1 | -isya / -isyek | kari → karisja / karisjek |
| 2 | -oi / -ok | kalam → kalamoi / kalamok |
| 3 | -wa / -wek | kirok → kirokwa / kirokwek |
| 4 | -an / -anik | sokis → sokisiin / sokisinik |
| 5 | -ai / -aik | mai → mai / maiik |
| 6 | -et / -etik | keri → ketik |

**Note:** Full class membership rules remain to be documented.

### 3.4 Loanword Integration

Loanwords from English and Kiswahili are treated as noun roots and assigned to the appropriate plural class.

| Loanword | Nandi Form | Source |
|----------|-----------|--------|
| suit | suutit | English |
| soap | saβunit | Kiswahili |
| box | βokisit | English |

### 3.5 Case

Nandi uses tonal case marking to distinguish nominative (subject) from oblique (object) case.

**Note:** Case marking via tone is not yet fully documented. This is a priority for future editions.

## Chapter 4: Verbs

### 4.1 Verb Classes

Nandi verbs fall into two main classes based on their infinitive prefix:

| Class | Infinitive Prefix | Example |
|-------|-------------------|---------|
| **Class I** | `ke-` | kesus (to bite) |
| **Class II** | `ki-` | kilul (to fell) |

A small number of verbs are irregular and do not follow either pattern.

### 4.2 Verb Structure

The general structure of a Nandi verb form is:

The tense prefix encodes both tense and subject. The object suffix encodes the object.

### 4.3 Present and Future Tense

Present and future tense are identical in form. Time words distinguish meaning.

**Class I (kesus — to bite)**

| Person | Form |
|--------|------|
| 1sg (I) | a-sus-e |
| 2sg (you) | i-sus-e |
| 3sg (he/she) | sus-e |
| 1pl (we) | ki-sus-e |
| 2pl (you all) | o-sus-e |
| 3pl (they) | sus-e |

**Class II (kilul — to fell)**

| Person | Form |
|--------|------|
| 1sg (I) | a-lul-ii |
| 2sg (you) | i-lul-ii |
| 3sg (he/she) | i-lul-i |
| 1pl (we) | ki-lul-ii |
| 2pl (you all) | o-lul-ii |
| 3pl (they) | i-lul-i |

**Note:** Class II 3rd person forms always carry the prefix `i-`.

**Future tense:** The same form is used. A time word such as `mutai` (tomorrow) marks futurity:

> `Nyone chii mutai.` — A person will come tomorrow.

### 4.4 Imperatives

**Class I:**
- Singular: root (e.g., `sus` — bite!)
- Plural: o- + root (e.g., `osus` — you all bite!)

**Class II:**
- Singular: i- + root (e.g., `ilul` — fell!)
- Plural: o- + root (e.g., `olul` — you all fell!)

### 4.5 Simple Past Tense

Simple past distinguishes three temporal levels: today, yesterday, day before yesterday and earlier.

**Class I — Simple Past of `kesus` (to bite)**

| Person | Today | Yesterday | Day Before+ |
|--------|-------|-----------|-------------|
| 1sg | ka-sus | koa-sus | kia-sus |
| 2sg | ke-sus | koi-sus | kii-sus |
| 3sg | ka-sus | ko-sus | ki-sus |
| 1pl | kaki-sus | koki-sus | kiki-sus |
| 2pl | ko-sus | koo-sus | kio-sus |
| 3pl | ka-sus | ko-sus | ki-sus |

**Class II — Simple Past of `kilul` (to fell)**

| Person | Today | Yesterday | Day Before+ |
|--------|-------|-----------|-------------|
| 1sg | ka-lul | koa-lul | kia-lul |
| 2sg | ke-lul | koi-lul | kii-lul |
| 3sg | ke-lul | koi-lul | kii-lul |
| 1pl | kaki-lul | koki-lul | kiki-lul |
| 2pl | ko-lul | koo-lul | kio-lul |
| 3pl | ke-lul | koi-lul | kii-lul |

The distinction between Class I and Class II falls on the 3rd person: Class I uses `ka-` / `ko-` / `ki-`, while Class II uses `ke-` / `koi-` / `kii-`.

### 4.6 Perfect Tense

The perfect also distinguishes three temporal levels.

**Perfect of `kesus` (to bite)**

| Person | Today | Yesterday | Day Before+ |
|--------|-------|-----------|-------------|
| 1sg | kaa-sus | koa-sus | kia-sus |
| 2sg | kai-sus | koi-sus | kii-sus |
| 3sg | kako-sus | koko-sus | kiko-sus |
| 1pl | kake-sus | koke-sus | kike-sus |
| 2pl | kao-sus | koo-sus | kio-sus |
| 3pl | kako-sus | koko-sus | kiko-sus |

### 4.7 Object Suffixes

Object suffixes attach directly to the verb root (or conjugated stem). They differ between present and past/perfect tenses.

**Object suffixes — Present Tense**

| Object | Suffix | Example (kesus) |
|--------|--------|-----------------|
| me | -ö | susö (it bites me) |
| you (sg) | -in | susin |
| him/her/it | -e (Class I) / -i (Class II) | suse / iluli |
| us | -ech | susech |
| you all | -ök | susök |
| them | -e / -i | suse / iluli |

**Object suffixes — Simple Past and Perfect**

| Object | Suffix | Example (kesus) |
|--------|--------|-----------------|
| me | -a | kasusa (he bit me) |
| you (sg) | -in | kasusin |
| him/her/it | (none) | kasus |
| us | -ech | kasusech |
| you all | -ak | kasusak |
| them | (none) | kasus |

**Example sentences:**

> `Kasusa chito rani.` — The person bit me today.
> `Kaasusin.` — I have bitten you.
> `Kakosusa.` — She has bitten me.

### 4.8 Irregular Verbs

Some verbs do not follow the regular patterns. Their full present paradigms are stored individually.

**kepwa — to come**

| Person | Form |
|--------|------|
| 1sg | anyone |
| 2sg | inyone |
| 3sg | nyone |
| 1pl | kipwane |
| 2pl | opwane |
| 3pl | pwane |

Imperative: `nyoo` (sg), `opwaa` (pl)

**kelapat — to run**

| Person | Form |
|--------|------|
| 1sg | alapatii |
| 2sg | ilapatii |
| 3sg | lapati |
| 1pl | kirwae |
| 2pl | orwae |
| 3pl | rwae |

Imperative: `lapat` (sg), `orwai` (pl)

**kepa — to go**

| Person | Form |
|--------|------|
| 1sg | awendi |
| 2sg | iwendi |
| 3sg | wendi |
| 1pl | kipendi |
| 2pl | opendi |
| 3pl | pendi |

Imperative: `ui` (sg), `opa` (pl)

### 4.9 Verb Derivation

**Agent nouns:** verb root + `-indet`

| Verb | Agent Noun | Gloss |
|------|-----------|-------|
| for- (steal) | forindet | thief |
| so:m- (beg) | somindet | beggar |

**Abstract nouns from adjectives:** adjective root + `-indo`

| Adjective | Abstract Noun | Gloss |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| tui- (black) | tuindo | blackness |
| kararan- (good) | kararaniado | goodness |

## Chapter 5: Word Order

### 5.1 Basic Word Order: VSO

Nandi's basic word order is Verb–Subject–Object.

> `Suse chito.` — The person bites.
> `Nyone chii mutai.` — A person will come tomorrow.

### 5.2 Time Words

Time words appear at the end of the sentence.

> `Kasusa chito rani.` — The person bit me today.

### 5.3 Object Position

When a sentence has both direct and indirect objects, the indirect object comes first.

## Workbook Chapter 1: Noun Definiteness

**Rule:** Every Nandi noun has an indefinite form ("a X") and a definite form ("the X"). The definite form is more common in speech.

**Examples:**

| Indefinite | Definite | Meaning |
|-----------|----------|---------|
| chii | chito | a person / the person |
| teta | tetab | a cow / the cow |
